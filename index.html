01 · Map

Where Arnold lives in USD

In Solaris there is no separate Arnold scene. Arnold reads the USD stage through its Hydra render delegate, and each kind of Arnold setting has its own home on that stage.

Click a box to see what goes there, which LOP writes it, and the USD it produces.

Rule of thumb: render-wide → RenderSettings prim (arnold:global:). Per object → primvars (primvars:arnold:), so they inherit down. Shaders → Material outputs (outputs:arnold:surface). Post-render and special shaders → node graphs.

03 · Operators

Arnold operators

Operators edit the Arnold scene at render time, after it has been built. The most used is set_parameter: select nodes with a pattern, then assign new values. It's how lighters in Maya or Katana override things they can't edit directly, such as data inside a procedural or an .ass file.

Other operators: set_transform (move nodes), disable (switch nodes off), collection (name a selection), include_graph (load operators from a file), merge and switch_operator (combine or choose between operator chains), and materialx (assign looks from a MaterialX document).

In Solaris, prefer LOP overrides. Edit Properties, Render Geometry Settings and Light Linker write USD opinions that appear in the Scene Graph, survive asset republishes and work in every renderer. Use operators for data USD can't reach: .ass files, Arnold procedurals, or pipeline-delivered operator graphs.

Operator support through Hydra has changed between HtoA versions; early Solaris builds didn't support operators at all. Check your HtoA version's "Supported Features" page, and remember the Diagnostics option ignore_operators switches every operator off for a test.

04 · Imagers
Imagers: post-processing inside Arnold

      
Imagers run on the finished image, before it's written: exposure, white balance, tone mapping, bloom, denoising and more. Because they run after rendering, you can tweak most of them during IPR without re-rendering the scene.

      
In USD they are written as a node graph and connected to the RenderSettings prim's imager input. HtoA writes imagers, light filters, AOV shaders and background or atmosphere shaders as node graphs, and applies imagers to all outputs.

      
Order matters. Each imager feeds the next. Denoise before bloom, or the bloom spreads noise. Tone map last, or every later step sees clipped values.

      
Denoisers: OIDN is fast and works on the beauty alone. Noice gives higher quality but needs extra AOVs and frames; it is often run as a separate pass after rendering.

    

      
      

      

      

    

  


  

    
05 · AOVs
AOVs and light groups

      
Each AOV is a RenderVar inside a RenderProduct. With Arnold, the beauty RenderVar can simply be named color. Other AOVs use Arnold's AOV names as the source name. A RenderVar's arnold:filter sets its pixel filter; the default is a box filter.

      
Light groups let comp rebalance lights without re-rendering. Give each light an AOV light group name (the Dome Light LOP's AOV Light Group menu lists the stage's lights), then request per-group AOVs such as RGBA_key.

07 · Debugging
Debugging an Arnold render

      
1. Read the log. Raise arnold:global:log:verbosity, or husk's -V. Missing textures, unknown shaders and license problems show up first.

      
2. Save what Arnold actually got. Set the environment variable HDARNOLD_DEBUG_SCENE to an .ass path. When a Hydra render starts, Arnold writes its scene there, and you can render it with kick, outside Houdini.

      
3. Stats and profiling. The Arnold Render Settings can write stats and profile files that show where time and memory went.

      
4. Bisect with ignore flags. The Diagnostics options switch whole features off: textures, displacement, SSS, subdivision, motion blur, atmosphere and more. Turn them on one at a time; the one that changes the render time the most is your suspect.

      
export HDARNOLD_DEBUG_SCENE=/tmp/sh010_debug.ass
husk -R HdArnoldRendererPlugin -V 6 -f 1001 -n 1 sh010.usd
kick -i /tmp/sh010_debug.ass -v 5

02 · Light placement
Place lights by clicking, not dragging

      
With a Light LOP selected, press Esc then Enter (or click Handles) to enter the light state. Instead of moving the light by hand, click where you want the result: the light moves itself.

      
Shift+D Diffuse: click a surface and the light faces it along the normal. Shift+S Specular: click where you want the highlight, as seen from the camera. Shift+F Shadow: click a pivot on the object, then Shift-click where its shadow should land. Shift+T Look at: orbit around a target. Shift+A back to handles.

      
In every mode, Ctrl-drag changes distance and Ctrl+Shift-drag changes exposure. Right-click for Keep Brightness, which keeps the lit surface just as bright while you move the light.

04 · Which renderer
The right renderer for the moment

      
Pick the renderer from the camera menu at the top right of the viewport (the "Persp" dropdown lists Houdini GL and every installed Hydra delegate). Switching is instant, so switch often: interactive Arnold is costly, while Houdini GL is nearly free.

      
Houdini 21 can keep several non-GL delegates alive at once (HOUDINI_ACTIVE_RENDER_DELEGATE_COUNT) and can delete delegates instead of pausing them when you switch away, which frees their memory.

    

  


  

    
05 · Versions
Render Gallery: compare and go back

      
Snap saves the current view as an EXR snapshot, together with the LOP network at that moment. Background starts a render that keeps updating its thumbnail while you keep working, and right-clicking it can send it to HQueue.

      
Set two snapshots as A and B to compare them: split, subtract, blend or highlight differences. Right-click a snapshot and choose Revert network to this snapshot to get back the exact setup that made it (Undo works if you change your mind).

      
Houdini 21's Live Render LOP links a render to the Render Gallery so it updates as upstream LOPs change.

    Chapter · Start here
    
Why lighters should care about USD

    
In a traditional pipeline, every department exports to its own formats: Alembic caches from animation, OBJ-level scenes in Houdini, then a renderer-specific scene file such as .ass or .ifd that nobody can easily read back. Each hand-off is a conversion, and each conversion can quietly lose something.

    
With USD, every department writes a layer into one shared scene description. Lighting reads the same stage that layout and animation wrote, adds its own layer on top, and hands that exact stage to the renderer through Hydra. What you inspect in Solaris is what the renderer receives.

    
For a lighter, the day-to-day shapes stay familiar: models, animation, FX and grooms still arrive as published assets. What changes is how you combine and override them, and that's what this lab teaches, from basic to advanced.

    
Studios moving to USD report the same lesson: USD has a higher barrier to entry, and it is very easy to make Solaris slow. The chapters on overrides, viewport optimization and debugging exist to get you past exactly that.

  

  

    

    

    

  






  

    Chapter 01 · The stage
    
Stage, prims and properties

    
A stage is the composed scene you see. It is built from one or more layers (files such as .usda or .usdc). The stage is a tree of prims, each addressed by a path like /World/Set/Table.

    
Each prim has a type (Xform, Mesh, Material, RectLight, Camera), a specifier and properties. Properties are either attributes, which hold values, or relationships, which point at other paths. Material binding is a relationship.

    
The specifier says what a layer is doing to the prim. def defines it, over only adds opinions to a prim defined somewhere else, and class makes an abstract prim that others inherit from. An over on its own is invisible to renderers.

    
Kind organizes assets in the hierarchy. An assembly is a set, a component is one asset such as a chair, and a subcomponent is a part inside it. Purpose decides who sees a prim: render for final frames, proxy for the viewport, guide for helpers.

    
Try it: click prims in the tree, then type any path in the parser below to see how USD splits it apart.

  

  

    

      

      

        

        

      

    

    

    

    

  






  

    Chapter 02 · Layer stacks
    
Layers stack, strongest on top

    
A stage opens one root layer, which lists its subLayers. The first sublayer listed is stronger than the next. For any attribute, USD walks the stack from the strongest layer down, and the first layer that has an opinion wins.

    
This is how departments share a shot without overwriting each other. Lighting sits above animation, which sits above layout, which sits above the asset. Each department writes only the changes it owns, in its own file.

    
Muting a layer removes it from composition without deleting anything. It's a quick way to ask "what did lighting change?"

    
Try it: click cells to add or remove opinions, mute layers, and reorder the stack. The bottom row shows the resolved value, colored by the layer that won.

    Chapter 03 · Composition arcs
    
LIVERPS strength order

    
Layers are not the only way opinions arrive. Composition arcs pull in opinions from other places, and when two arcs disagree, USD applies a fixed order of strength, strongest first:

    
Local, Inherits, Variant sets, rElocates, References, Payloads, Specializes.

    
The order was long known as LIVRPS. Relocates was added more recently, which made it LIVERPS. Relocates lets a referenced prim be moved to a new namespace location.

    
The order applies recursively. A reference brings in a whole layer stack, and inside that stack the same rules apply again. That is how an asset can have its own variants and classes while a shot still overrides them.

    
Two patterns are worth remembering. Inherits are stronger than references, so editing a class in the shot updates every chair at once. Specializes is the weakest arc, so it works as a fallback default that anything else can override.

    Chapter 04 · Variant sets
    
One asset, many versions

    
A variant set is a named switch on a prim. Each variant holds a bundle of opinions, and only the selected one composes. A chair can have a modelVariant (wood, metal, plastic), a lod (high, proxy) and a shadingVariant (clean, worn).

    
The selection is itself an opinion, so it obeys layer strength. The asset sets a default, layout picks per chair, and a stronger shot layer can still change it.

    
In Solaris you author variants with Add Variant or the Component Builder's variant inputs, and you pick them with Set Variant. Variant sets nest, so a shadingVariant can live inside each modelVariant.

  

  

    

      
      

        

        

        

        
        

      

    

    

  






  

    Chapter 07 · Materials
    
Binding and render contexts

    
Materials are prims, usually under /materials. Geometry points at one with the material:binding relationship. A binding on a parent applies to all its children, and the nearest binding normally wins.

    
A parent can flip that with bindMaterialAs = "strongerThanDescendants". Collection-based bindings (material:binding:collection:<name>) assign one material to a set of paths in one go; this is what Assign Material does with path patterns.

    
Bindings can have a purpose. preview bindings feed fast viewport shaders, while full bindings are used for final rendering.

    
A single Material can hold networks for several renderers at once through render contexts. Arnold looks for outputs:arnold:surface, MaterialX-aware renderers look for outputs:mtlx:surface, and everyone falls back to the universal outputs:surface, normally a UsdPreviewSurface.

    Chapter 08 · Solaris
    
How LOPs write USD

    
Every LOP outputs a complete, composed stage. Each node copies the stage it receives, edits only the prims it touches, and passes the result on. The network is a chain of edits, not a list of objects.

    
Most nodes write into the active layer, the strongest in-memory layer in the stack. When the strongest layer is a file loaded from disk, the next editing node creates an implicit layer above it. Files from disk are never edited in place; your changes always land in a layer above.

    
A Layer Break starts a fresh layer, and marks everything above it as already authored. The viewport still shows the whole scene, but the USD ROP throws away what came before the break when it saves, so only your overrides end up in the file.

    
Configure Layer gives the active layer a save path, so it goes to its own file. Solaris uses these nodes in place of USD's Edit Target. Turn on any node's debug flag to put its edits in their own layer for inspection.

    
Try it: bypass nodes and move the Layer Break, and watch the layer stack and the files written to disk change.

    Chapter · Render settings
    
Arnold sampling, explained with rays

    
Arnold's settings live on the RenderSettings prim in the arnold:global: namespace. The most important idea is how samples multiply. Camera (AA) samples are squared to get rays per pixel. Every other sample count is also squared, then multiplied by the AA rays.

    
So AA 3 with diffuse 2 fires 9 camera rays and 9 × 4 = 36 diffuse rays per pixel. Raising AA by one step raises the cost of everything else too. That's why the usual approach is to set AA for edges, motion blur and depth of field, then raise only the ray type whose AOV is noisy.

    
Adaptive sampling lets pixels that are still noisy keep sampling up to AA_samples_max, which saves time on clean areas. Ray depth limits how many bounces each ray type gets. Clamping tames fireflies at the cost of a little energy.

    Chapter · Render settings
    
Resolution, crop and overscan

    
The RenderSettings prim says which camera and how many pixels. A few more attributes do a lot of everyday lighting work.

    
dataWindowNDC sets which part of the frame is rendered, in 0–1 screen coordinates. Values inside 0–1 make a crop region for quick tests. Values outside 0–1 render overscan, extra image around the frame for comp distortion and camera shake.

    
pixelAspectRatio handles anamorphic plates. aspectRatioConformPolicy decides what happens when the camera aperture and the image shape disagree. disableMotionBlur and instantaneousShutter switch motion blur off for a quick test without touching the camera.

    Chapter · Overrides
    
The lighter's override toolbox

    
Lighting is mostly overriding: hiding things, tweaking an asset's light, changing render settings on one object. In USD, an override is just a stronger opinion in your layer. The asset files never change, so when the asset is republished, your override still applies on top.

    
Two things matter for every override: how it is written (set, block, multiply…) and which node writes it. Choosing the right node keeps the stage fast and the next artist unsurprised.

    Chapter · Passes
    
Render layers, mattes and holdouts

    
Comp usually wants the shot split up: characters, environment, FX. In Solaris you branch the same stage once per pass, apply per-pass overrides, and give each branch its own RenderSettings and render node. Because overrides are layers, the passes share everything else and stay consistent.

    
Each object group in a pass takes one of four states:

    
Visible renders normally. Matte (holdout) renders as a black alpha hole so things behind it are cut out. Phantom is invisible to the camera but still casts shadows and shows in reflections. Hidden is removed from the render entirely.

    
Prefer visibility, purposes or payloads to hide things. Deactivating prims forces heavy recomposition in big scenes.

    Chapter 09 · Rendering
    
Hydra, render settings and husk

    
Hydra is USD's rendering framework. The stage is translated into a scene that any render delegate can draw: Houdini GL, Storm, Karma CPU, Karma XPU, Arnold, RenderMan and others. Houdini 21 made Hydra 2 (scene indices) the standard interface, husk included.

    
What to render is described in USD itself. A RenderSettings prim sets the camera, resolution and the products to make. Each RenderProduct is one output file. Each RenderVar is one AOV inside it, either a raw render output or a Light Path Expression.

    
husk is the command-line renderer that ships with Houdini. It renders any USD file through any delegate. It's what a Karma or USD Render ROP runs on the farm.

  

  

    

      

      

      

    

    

    

    

    
  






  

    Chapter 05 · Scale
    
Payloads, instancing and purpose

    
A payload is a reference you can unload. With a payload unloaded, the stage keeps the asset's top prim but none of its contents, so a city of 20,000 buildings opens in seconds. In Solaris, the viewport's load-masks and the Configure Stage LOP control what is loaded.

    
Native instancing (instanceable = true) tells USD that identical referenced assets can share one prototype. The scene graph collapses and renderers store the mesh once.

    
A PointInstancer goes further. One prim holds arrays of positions, orientations, scales and prototype indices, with no prim per copy. It's the format for scattered rocks, trees and crowds.

    
Purpose keeps heavy geometry out of the viewport. Show the proxy while you work and render with render; guide prims never render.

    Chapter 06 · Time
    
Defaults, time samples and strength

    
An attribute can have a default value and any number of timeSamples. Between samples, USD interpolates linearly by default. With interpolationType = "held" on the stage, it holds the last sample instead, which suits stepped animation.

    
Within one layer, time samples beat the default. Across layers, though, strength comes first: a stronger layer's plain default beats a weaker layer's animation. That catches people out when a lighting layer accidentally freezes an animated light.

    
Long caches are often split into value clips, many small per-frame files that the stage stitches together over time. Houdini's Geometry Clip Sequence LOP builds these, and the clip data sits below any timeSamples authored in the layer that holds the clip metadata.

    Chapter · Performance
    
Keeping Solaris fast

    
Solaris re-cooks every node between a change and the display flag, re-composes the stage where needed, then Hydra translates the result for the viewport. Slowness almost always comes from one of three places: cooking too much, composing too much, or drawing too much.

    
The simulator starts from an unoptimized environment shot. Turn on each fix and see which cost it attacks. The numbers are illustrative; the relative effects follow SideFX's performance guidance.

    
A quick check: the LOP layer colors in the network editor show where new layers start. Many color changes means many layers, and maybe many stages to compose.

    Chapter 10 · Arnold in Solaris
    
Arnold through USD

    
HtoA brings Arnold into Solaris with two parts of arnold-usd: the render delegate (HdArnold), which renders the stage, and the node registry plugin, which makes Arnold shaders known to USD so Material Library can build them.

    
Lights are standard UsdLux prims, translated to Arnold nodes at render time. Arnold-only settings use the arnold: namespace, usually as primvars on geometry so they inherit down the hierarchy.

    
One trap: UsdLux shaping:cone:angle is the half angle, measured from the axis to the edge. Arnold's cone_angle is the full angle. 45° in USD is a 90° Arnold spot.

    
Current at the time of writing: HtoA 6.5.2.0 with Arnold 7.5.2.0 (June 2026), supporting Houdini 20.0, 20.5 and 21.0. It adds live Copernicus textures in Solaris materials and preliminary reading of the USD 26.03 Gaussian splat schema.

    Chapter · Pipeline
    
How studios structure USD shots

    
Studios moving from a legacy pipeline to USD tend to arrive at similar building blocks. Names differ from studio to studio; the ideas are shared.

    
Asset descriptions act like a mini scene description for one asset. A rock asset might combine a model with lookdev, a moss groom with lookdev, and FX elements, loaded as one unit.

    
Assemblies group many assets into an environment. They load version locked for stability, and can be unlocked to version individual components without republishing the whole set.

    
Environment overrides are done procedurally in the shot: any part of a huge set can be changed with USD opinions, without unlocking HDAs or republishing.

    
Lighting and lookdev packages ship as USD layers. Multi-shot workflows render many shots from one workspace, with a master setup and per-shot children. Houdini 21's Shot Builder (beta) is SideFX's take on this.

    
Render-time procedurals, such as fur curves generated in husk, need the asset resolver to find their data on the farm, so a custom resolver is often part of the render setup.
